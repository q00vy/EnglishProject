Cramming vs. Spaced Repetition: How First-Year Students Can Remember English Words

Author: Matvey Stoilskiy • University of Tyumen • 2026

1. The Problem & Research Question

Every week, first-year university students need to learn 30 to 50 new English words. Most students prepare for vocabulary tests using "cramming" — reading a word list many times the night before class. As a result, they pass the test, but two days later they forget almost everything. Students waste study time and lose motivation.

Research Question: How can first-year students remember English vocabulary longer without spending extra study time?

2. Scientific Background

Cognitive psychology explains why cramming fails. According to the Ebbinghaus Forgetting Curve (replicated by Murre and Dros in 2015), our brain deletes information that we do not review. In addition, psychologist Nate Kornell (2009) compared cramming and spaced repetition among university students and found a clear gap between what students choose and what actually works:

3. Student Survey & 7-Day Mini-Experiment

To confirm that this problem is real at the University of Tyumen, I conducted a 5-question survey among 20 first-year students in my group and ran a 7-day vocabulary experiment.

7-Day Mini-Experiment: I tested two sets of 15 unfamiliar English words (Level B1) using equal total study time (20 minutes):

  • List A (Cramming): Studied for 20 minutes in one evening.
  • List B (Spaced Repetition + Context): Studied for 5 minutes per day on Day 1, Day 2, Day 4, and Day 7 in short example sentences.

4. Comparison of Learning Methods

Method Study Routine Day 7 Retention Main Result
1. Cramming Reading a word list 30–40 times in one evening. Low (~25–30%) Works only for a test the next morning; fast forgetting.
2. Spaced Repetition Short reviews on Day 1, Day 2, Day 4, and Day 7. High (~75–80%) Moves words into long-term memory with the same study time.
3. Context + Repetition Learning words in real sentences + spaced intervals. Highest (~80%+) Helps students use new words actively in speaking and writing.

5. Practical Solution: 3-Step Guide for Students

Step 1: Replace word lists with short sentences
Never learn isolated translations. Write one simple sentence connected to your studies or daily life so your brain builds a logical link.
Step 2: Follow the 1–2–4–7 repetition schedule
Review new vocabulary after 24 hours, then on Day 2, Day 4, and Day 7. Free apps like Anki or Quizlet calculate these intervals automatically.
Step 3: Connect English to your real interests
Read short posts or watch videos about topics you already enjoy (technology, sports, music) to meet new words naturally in real context.
